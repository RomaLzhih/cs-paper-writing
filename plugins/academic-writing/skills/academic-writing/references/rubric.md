# Extraction rubric

Every paper in the corpus is read against these eight dimensions. The rubric exists so
that observations from different papers land in the same bins and can be counted, rather
than accumulating as unrelated notes.

Read for **craft, not content**. The question is never "what did they find?" but "what did
they do to the prose so a reader could follow the finding?" A pattern is only worth
recording if it would survive being lifted into a paper on an unrelated topic.

## A. Macro-architecture

How the whole document is shaped before any paragraph is written.

- Title/abstract contract: what the title promises, whether the abstract discharges it.
- Abstract move sequence and the budget (sentences) spent on each move.
- Introduction move sequence: territory -> gap -> occupation, and its variants.
- Section inventory and the *job* each section is asked to do (not its label).
- Where contributions are stated, in what grammatical form, and how many.
- Roadmap and forward-reference sentences: present or absent, and where.
- What is deliberately deferred (to appendix, to later section) and how the deferral is signposted.

## B. Section-level rhetorical moves

- **Introduction**: how the gap is constructed (contradiction, absence, insufficiency,
  unexamined assumption); how the contribution list is grammatically framed.
- **Related work**: organizing axis (chronological / thematic / by-dimension-of-comparison);
  the device used to avoid a laundry list; whether prior work is positioned as rival,
  foundation, or complement.
- **Method**: overview-before-detail ordering; where notation is introduced; whether
  motivation precedes mechanism; how design choices are justified in-line.
- **Results**: the claim -> evidence -> interpretation unit; verbs used to reference
  tables and figures; how magnitude is characterized in words alongside numbers.
- **Discussion / limitations**: what is conceded and how early; hedging calibration;
  whether alternative interpretations are raised by the authors or left to the reader.
- **Conclusion**: what it restates, what it adds, what it refuses to add.

## C. Paragraph craft

- Topic-sentence discipline: does sentence 1 carry the paragraph's claim?
- Paragraph shape: claim -> support -> implication, and its variants.
- Length norms (sentences per paragraph) and where the norm is broken deliberately.
- Given-new flow: does each sentence open with information the previous one established?
- Transition inventory: the actual connective words and phrases used, and their frequency.
- One-idea-per-paragraph adherence, and how a violated paragraph is repaired by the authors.

## D. Sentence craft

- Length distribution: mean, and how variance is used for emphasis.
- Subject-verb proximity; depth of embedded clauses.
- Nominalization: whether actions stay verbs.
- Active/passive distribution, and specifically *what work passive is chosen to do*.
- Parallelism in lists and coordinated clauses.
- Claim-verb inventory: the verbs carrying assertions (show, suggest, indicate, observe,
  find, demonstrate) and the epistemic strength each is reserved for.
- Hedge and booster lexicon, and whether strength tracks evidence.

## E. Precision and terminology

- Term-introduction protocol: definition on first use, then invariant reuse.
- One term per concept; no elegant variation on technical vocabulary.
- Notation conventions and when symbols replace words.
- Treatment of vague quantifiers (many, significant, substantially) - avoided or anchored.
- Antecedent clarity for pronouns and demonstratives ("this" + noun).

## F. Citation integration

- Integral vs non-integral citation, and what governs the choice.
- Citation function: support, contrast, grounding, attribution of a method, or of a gap.
- Density and placement within the paragraph.
- How the boundary between what is cited and what is claimed is kept visible.
- How a body of work is summarized in one sentence without flattening it.

## G. Evidence presentation

- Coupling distance between a claim and the evidence for it.
- Number-reporting conventions: precision, units, uncertainty, baselines for comparison.
- Caption self-containedness: can a figure or table be read standing alone?
- Honesty markers: negative results, failed variants, scope limits stated in the open.
- How a result that does not support the thesis is reported.

## H. Reader management

- Signposting density and phrasing.
- Explicit statements of why a section or choice matters.
- Anticipation of objections, and where they are placed relative to the claim.
- Cognitive-load control: example before formalism, running examples, naming of concepts.
- What the authors assume the reader already knows, and how that assumption is signalled.
